Bonus: it can be made exact

Average the potential over the segment

The centered difference was the only approximation. Move it into the potential instead. Define the looking-glass potential $\hat V$, the average of $V$ between the two branch points $x \pm \hat p/2$:

$$\hat V(x,\hat p) = \int_{-1/2}^{1/2} V(x+\sigma\hat p)\,d\sigma \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \hat p\,\frac{\partial\hat V}{\partial x} = V\!\left(x+\tfrac{\hat p}{2}\right) - V\!\left(x-\tfrac{\hat p}{2}\right)\ \text{exactly.}$$
  • Schrödinger in $V$ = Liouville in $\hat V$, with no remainder. The force is averaged over the coherence length $\hat p$.
  • Leading order: $\delta\hat V = \hat V - V \approx \frac{\hat p^2}{24}V''$. That is the centered difference and its $\hbar^2$ term from slide 10.
  • $V$ stays an ordinary potential. The correction $\delta\hat V$ lives in the Fourier picture; inverse-transform it to a kernel $\delta V(x,q)$ over momentum transfer $q$. The dynamics is still a flux, so $w \to w + c$ stays invisible.
  • How big is $\delta\hat V$? It depends on the coherence length, not just $\hbar$. Two examples follow.

The integral form of the mean-value theorem: averaging the force over the segment gives exactly the difference of the potential at the ends. Carnovali and França use this step but take the endpoints together; keeping the segment finite makes it an exact change of potential.

Harmonic: $\delta\hat V$ doesn't depend on $x$, so it exerts no force. Quartic: the series stops after the $\hbar^2$ term.
