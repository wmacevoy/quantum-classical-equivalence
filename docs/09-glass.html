Separate variables

Through the looking glass, Schrödinger appears

Try a product, $\hat w = \psi^+(z_+,t)\,\psi^-(z_-,t)$. Each factor obeys a Schrödinger equation:

$$i\hbar\,\psi^-_t = H\,\psi^-, \qquad -i\hbar\,\psi^+_t = H\,\psi^+$$

So $\psi^- = \Psi$, the ordinary wavefunction, and $\psi^+ = \Psi^*$, its time-reversed mirror image:

$$\hat w(z_+, z_-, t) = \Psi^*(z_+,t)\;\Psi(z_-,t)$$

In general $\hat w$ is a sum of such products, $\sum C_{\lambda\lambda'}\,e^{i(\lambda-\lambda')t}\,\psi_\lambda^*(z_+)\,\psi_{\lambda'}(z_-)$, and the coefficients $C$ form the density matrix.

So $w(x,p,t)$ is…

…exactly the Wigner function (Wigner, 1932): quantum mechanics in phase space.

Honesty about novelty

The mathematics is classic (Wigner, Groenewold, Moyal). The Liouville → Schrödinger route was published by Carnovali & França (2005). What's offered here is the direction of reading, classical → quantum, and what that reading buys.

The "looking glass" is the pair of mirrored coordinates: one copy of the wavefunction runs forward in time, its conjugate runs backward.

The usual textbook direction is quantum to classical: start with Ψ, build W, take ħ → 0. Here we start from the classical side.
