Idea 1

Any ODE lifts to a linear PDE

Any ODE

$$\frac{d\mathbf u}{dt} = \mathbf v(\mathbf u), \qquad \mathbf u \in \mathbb R^n$$

Smear the initial condition into a density $w(\mathbf u,t)$. Probability is conserved and moves with velocity $\mathbf v$, so it obeys a continuity equation, like mass in a fluid:

$$\frac{\partial w}{\partial t} + \nabla_{\mathbf u}\cdot(\mathbf v\,w) = 0$$
  • Linear in $w$, however nonlinear $\mathbf v$ is.
  • $w_0 = \delta(\mathbf u - \mathbf u_0)$ rides the trajectory: nothing lost.

Apply it to Hamilton

Newton is second order in $x$, so a first-order system needs two numbers per degree of freedom: the initial data. Hamilton's choice is $\mathbf u = (x,p)$:

$$\dot x = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p}, \qquad \dot p = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}$$

That's why $w$ lives on phase space. The flow has zero divergence, $\partial_x(\partial_p H) + \partial_p(-\partial_x H) = 0$, so:

$$w_t + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\,w_x - \frac{\partial H}{\partial x}\,w_p = 0$$

With $H = \frac{p^2}{2M} + V(x)$ this is the Liouville equation.

The general idea: an ODE moves points; a PDE moves a density of points. Derive the continuity equation the same way as in fluids: the rate of change of probability in a fixed box equals the net flux v·w through its walls. Fritz John's PDE text presents it as the equation for the distribution of initial values.

Why x and p, and not x alone? Because Newton's law is second order, the state is the pair (x, p): exactly the initial data you need. That is the space the density has to live on.

Why p and not velocity? With canonical momentum the flow is divergence-free (Liouville's theorem), so the cloud's phase-space volume is conserved and v·∇w is the whole equation. Hamilton's equations hand us exactly that.
